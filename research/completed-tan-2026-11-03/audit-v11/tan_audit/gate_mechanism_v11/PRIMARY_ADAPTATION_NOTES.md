# Primary adaptation notes for prospective v11

Literature-only review, 2026-11-02. No simulations, estimators, measured outcomes, repository edits, or design selection. 

Ordinary adaptation already supplies a concrete alternative explanation for onset transients and preferential responses to rapid changes. Changing temporal correlation while preserving marginal variance is a useful challenge to a fixed model, but its outcome is not by itself a signature of TAN attention or a universally directional test of adaptation.

## Primary sources and exact scope

**Benda, Longtin & Maler (2005), “Spike-Frequency Adaptation Separates Transient Communication Signals from Background Oscillations,” J Neurosci 25:2312–2321.** In Methods, “Adaptation model,” Eq.1a is `f(t)=f0(I(t)−A(t))`; adaptation is subtractive negative feedback driven by firing frequency. Eq.3 gives a single-pole high-pass gain for linear onset/steady-state firing-rate curves, with `tau_eff=tau*f'_infinity/f'_0`. Slow components have lower gain than fast components. Their electroreceptor experiments demonstrate preferential responses to rapid chirps over slower background oscillations. Importantly, Eq.4 and Results explain that spike generation adds temporal averaging/low-pass filtering; adaptation-only gain substantially overpredicts responses without that stage. The linear approximation depends on the operating regime and timescale separation. The fitted biological cutoff is not a parameter prescription for dimensionless Task B. [Original full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6726095/).

**Benda, Maler & Longtin (2010), “Linear Versus Nonlinear Signal Transmission in Neuron Models With Adaptation Currents or Dynamic Thresholds,” J Neurophysiol 104:2806–2820.** Results, “Transfer functions,” Fig.3, and “Averaging theory,” Eqs.17–20: both adaptation currents and dynamic thresholds produce high-pass components, despite similar adapting step responses. An adaptation current shifts adapted onset `f–I` curves through `f(I,A)=f0(I−A)`; a dynamic threshold changes gain divisively in the models studied. Its gain and filtering depend more strongly on mean input. Therefore matched onset/steady responses do not establish equivalence across stimuli, and a spike-triggered threshold baseline does not represent every standard negative-feedback mechanism. The transfer demonstration used weak, low-pass-filtered Gaussian current to limit nonlinear effects; it is not a universal strong-noise or detection theorem. [Original publisher full text](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/jn.00240.2010), DOI `10.1152/jn.00240.2010`.

**Fontaine, Peña & Brette (2014), “Spike-Threshold Adaptation Predicted by Membrane Potential Dynamics In Vivo,” PLoS Comput Biol 10:e1003560.** The unnumbered threshold equation in Results p.2 and Methods “Adaptive threshold model,” p.9 is `tau_theta*dtheta/dt=theta_infinity(V_m)−theta`; threshold follows voltage rather than requiring previous output spikes. Slow voltage changes are followed by threshold, leaving a faster effective signal `V_m−theta`. Results around Fig.6 explicitly say this can contribute to a single onset spike, but need not do so because reset adds rapid voltage changes. This provides a primary example of ordinary subthreshold adaptation producing change sensitivity. It concerns particular auditory neurons; it does not validate TAN biologically. Fig.2 and Methods use OU inputs, including changed autocorrelation, to test parameter recovery, not to prove a general fixed-variance detection ranking. [Original publisher article](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003560), [original PDF](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003560&type=printable).

**Franzen, Ramlow & Lindner (2023; online 2022), “The steady state and response to a periodic stimulation of the firing rate for a theta neuron with correlated noise,” J Comput Neurosci 51:107–128.** Model Eq.5 specifies `tau*deta/dt=−eta+sqrt(2*tau*sigma²)*xi`, with covariance `sigma²*exp(−|lag|/tau)` and intensity `D=tau*sigma²`. Section “Constant variance scaling,” Fig.3 and Eqs.36–39 show that changing correlation time at fixed variance can increase, decrease, or nonmonotonically change firing rate in an ordinary theta/QIF neuron. Fixed intensity and fixed variance are different interventions; their white-noise limits differ. This model has no adaptation: it directly cautions against an architecture-general spike-rate sign, without predicting TAN, AdaptiveLIF, or step-detection performance. [Original full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9840600/), [publisher DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10827-022-00836-6).

## What follows for the proposed comparison

**Spectral expectation, explicitly conditional:** shifting equal-variance noise toward slower components should reduce its contribution through a fixed linear high-pass stage, relative to faster components in the filter's rising-gain region. This follows from the adaptation transfer functions above. It does not imply monotonic false-spike rate, preserved step detection, or a TAN advantage after leak, saturation, reset and threshold. These stages can introduce low-pass effects and operating-point changes. “Noise rejection” still requires a separate retained-signal endpoint.

**Application to the specified TAN, by algebra rather than a literature claim:** its residual has FIR transfer `H_S(z)=1−(1/W)*sum_{j=0}^{W−1}z^(−j)`, hence zero DC gain once the window is full. Sensitivity to temporal correlation can therefore arise before attention, from ordinary subtraction of a moving mean. This is not a proof that the whole neuron is a linear high-pass filter: `tanh(abs(S))`, signed-query content weighting, clipping, membrane integration and reset remain nonlinear. Use no biological millisecond/Hz identification without a defined timestep.

**Interpretation limit for one train-calibrated test:** freeze calibration objectives, allowed baseline parameters, calibration data and the readout before the correlation-shift test. Matching activity alone can favor silence; preserve the signal-detection tradeoff. A held-out difference supports a contrast between the specified calibrated models under that shift. It does not exclude all adaptive-threshold/negative-feedback models or causally localize a difference to TAN's attention/gate without an appropriate within-model intervention. Conversely, baseline success would show that ordinary adaptation is sufficient for that measured behavior, not that every TAN component is redundant.

No new result is asserted. The source support is for existing mechanisms and bounded expectations, not generic adaptation novelty.
